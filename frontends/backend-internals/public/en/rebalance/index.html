Working the dials

The two bars are the inequality. When the top one (batch processing time) exceeds the bottom one (max.poll.interval.ms) it turns red, and from that moment you are dropped from the group on every single batch. Not probabilistically, and not as a function of load — the arithmetic says so.

The strip below shows where the time goes in one cycle. The red span is time spent processing records that were never committed. At the default settings the whole strip is red or grey.

There is no randomness. Everything is closed-form, so the same dial settings always produce the same numbers.

Try this

1. Effective throughput is exactly zero — and the CPU is 98% busy

max.poll.records 500 · 700ms per record · max.poll.interval 5m batch processing time 350s > limit 300s records processed before the deadline 428 · of those, committed 0.0% effective throughput 0.00 rec/s · wasted 4.21 rec/s · consumer busy 98.4%

500 × 700ms = 350 seconds. It cannot finish inside five minutes. At the 300-second mark the coordinator drops this consumer from the group, and the consumer — not knowing that — keeps going to 428 records. And those 428 records are never committed, because auto-commit happens inside poll() and it never got back to poll(). Not near zero. Exactly zero.

2. Scale to twelve consumers to catch up — twelve times the waste

consumers 3 → 12 effective throughput 0.00 → 0.00 rec/s wasted 4.21 → 16.84 rec/s

The lag is growing, so you add consumers. The most natural reaction there is. And effective throughput stays at zero. Every one of them is crossing the same inequality, so twelve consumers each process 428 records and each throw them away. All you added is waste and broker load.

3. Raise max.poll.interval to an hour — the loop stops, but

max.poll.interval 5m → 1h effective throughput 0.00 → 4.29 rec/s failure-detection delay 300s → 3600s

The inequality holds, the loop stops, throughput comes back. The most common fix, and the worst one. That value is also "how long before we decide a consumer is dead". At an hour, a consumer that has genuinely hung goes unnoticed for an hour — and nobody consumes its partitions for that hour.

4. Cut max.poll.records to 100 instead — same gain, no cost

interval back to 5m · max.poll.records 500 → 100 batch processing time 350s → 70s effective throughput 4.29 rec/s (same as recipe 3) · detection delay still 300s

You fixed the other variable of the same inequality. Throughput is identical to recipe 3 and the detection delay did not move, because max.poll.records is only a batch size and has nothing to do with detection. Both satisfy the inequality; the prices differ — shrink the left side.

5. Commit every 10 records — no longer zero, still looping

max.poll.records back to 500 (inequality violated) · commit every 10 commit ratio 0.0% → 98.1% · effective throughput 0.00 → 4.13 rec/s rebalances still 0.20/min

The 420 records processed before the deadline now get committed, and throughput is alive. But you did not fix the inequality. You are still dropped every cycle, a rebalance still runs, and everyone still stops for five seconds. You are making progress while being repeatedly evicted — this is first aid.

6. Inequality satisfied — now deploys are the problem (eager)

max.poll.records 100 (inequality holds) · churn 6/min · eager effective throughput 4.29 → 2.14 rec/s consumer busy 50.0%

Frequent deploys, or one flapping consumer, mean frequent rebalances. With eager assignment, one rebalance stops everyone. Six times five seconds is thirty seconds — half of every minute is a stall. You stop even when none of your partitions move.

7. Switch to cooperative-sticky — the blast radius shrinks, nothing else

assignor eager → cooperative effective throughput 2.14 → 3.57 rec/s (+66.7%) but go back to recipe 1's settings and it is zero, same as eager

Only the partitions that must move are revoked, so the rest keep consuming and the loss is divided by the consumer count. And in a state where the inequality is violated it can do nothing — everyone is being dropped, so every partition has to move, and cooperative becomes eager. Check the comparison table.

The cause is not feedback, it is an inequality

The name "rebalance loop" suggests something that snowballs. The primary cause is a single static inequality.

max.poll.records × per-record time > max.poll.interval.ms
While that is true you are dropped on every batch, deterministically. At low load too. With a single consumer too. And you never escape on your own until the inequality becomes false.

Which makes it different from the metastability in no. 14. There, shedding load could bring you back under the threshold. Here, you could drop the load to zero and the very next batch drops you again. The exit exists only in the configuration.

The heartbeat is fine and you are out of the group

This is the most confusing part. Consumer liveness runs on two separate tracks.

What it measuresWho sends itDoes long processing trip it?
session.timeout.ms (default 45s)A background thread, every heartbeat.interval.ms (default 3s)No. However long the processing runs, heartbeats keep going out normally
max.poll.interval.ms (default 5m)The application thread, by calling poll() at allYes. This is the one

So the logs leave you with what looks like a contradiction: the consumer is alive and it is out of the group. The thread that sends heartbeats is perfectly healthy; the only problem is that poll() was never reached. Raising session.timeout.ms does nothing for this.

What this model leaves out

WhatWhy it is out
KIP-848, the new group protocolThe broker drives the rebalance and stop-the-world goes away. This page's inequality survives; the blast radius of D changes again — check the docs for the version you run
Skewed partitionsConsumers are assumed uniform. In practice only the consumer holding the heavy partition is dropped, and that is exactly where cooperative earns the most
The distribution of processing timePer-record time is a constant here. With a long tail the inequality becomes true sometimes, so you are dropped intermittently — a much harder shape to find
Reprocessing feeding backThe cost of re-processing an uncommitted batch is counted in the waste meter, but its second-order effect of pushing the lag further up is not modelled
