What "exactly once" actually costs

Use QoS 2 and your message arrives exactly once. That sentence is true, but where the promise ends and where you pay for it rarely come up. Let us run all three levels side by side.

0 %
three levels, one set of conditions

QoS 0at most once

Fire and forget. No acknowledgement, no retransmission.

QoS 1at least once

Resends until acknowledged — which is why it can arrive twice.

QoS 2exactly once

Four exchanges to remove duplicates. Slower and more expensive.

All three lanes consume one packet per tick, at the same rate. So how many messages each finishes in the same time is exactly what that level costs. The chips above are packets just exchanged (red strikethrough is lost, dashed is a timeout wait); the cells below are per-message outcomes — an empty green cell is one delivery, × is lost, 2 is a duplicate. Grey numbers in the readouts are the expected values for these conditions.

Things to try

1. At 0% failure, just count packets

failure rate 0 QoS 0 1 packet per message QoS 1 2 packets per message QoS 2 4 packets per message all three lanes: zero loss, zero duplicates

On a network that loses nothing, all three levels produce the same result. The only difference is the bill. QoS 2 costs four times QoS 0 even when nothing goes wrong. Notice how differently fast the lanes advance.

2. Raise it to 20% — this is the heart of the page

failure rate 20 QoS 0 lost 20% 1.00 packets QoS 1 duplicated 25% 2.81 packets QoS 2 no loss, no duplicates 5.63 packets

Each level pays in its own currency. QoS 0 loses messages, QoS 1 delivers them twice, QoS 2 burns twice the packets. None of the three options is free.

Note that QoS 1's duplicate rate is higher than the failure rate: 25% duplicates at 20% failure. A retransmission fires when either leg of the round trip fails.

3. Push it to 40%

failure rate 40 QoS 1 duplicated 66.7% 4.44 packets QoS 2 no duplicates 8.89 packets QoS 2 lost 0.8% hit the 12-retransmission cap QoS 2's outcome cells fill noticeably slower

QoS 2 now spends close to nine packets per message. Duplicates are still zero, but throughput collapses and around 0.8% of messages are abandoned outright on hitting the retransmission cap. The price of exactly-once climbs steeply as the network degrades — and the awkward part is that a degraded network is precisely when you have to pay it.

4. Read the QoS 1 chips one line at a time

hit pause and look at the QoS 1 lane PUBLISH → subscriber → PUBACK(struck out) → timeout → PUBLISH → subscriber (duplicate) the message had already arrived, and it was sent again

The moment a duplicate is born is right there. The subscriber already has it; only the publisher does not know that. The acknowledgement vanished. From the publisher's side, "the PUBLISH was lost" and "the PUBACK was lost" look identical. With no way to tell them apart, resending is the only move available.

5. Wait for a PUBCOMP to be lost in the QoS 2 lane

watch QoS 2 at 30–40% failure PUBREL → subscriber → PUBCOMP(struck out) → timeout → PUBREL → no delivery the PUBREL went again, but no subscriber chip appears

This is how QoS 2 removes duplicates. The broker discards the packet id as it processes the PUBREL. When the same PUBREL arrives again there is nothing left to match, so it replies PUBCOMP without delivering. The retransmission still happens — the duplicate delivery does not.

What each level promises

The three levels are different promises about how many times a message arrives.

levelpromiseon failure
QoS 0at most onceit simply disappears
QoS 1at least onceresend → it may arrive twice
QoS 2exactly onceresend, but filter the duplicate

The names are precise. QoS 1 is not "once" but "at least once". Duplicates are not an exceptional condition — they are normal, designed-in behaviour. If you choose QoS 1, handling duplicates is not optional.

Why QoS 1 produces duplicates

The publisher sends a PUBLISH and waits for a PUBACK. If none arrives in time, it sends again. The problem is that "none arrived" has two possible causes.

① the PUBLISH was lost on the way out   → the subscriber never got it → resending is right
② the PUBACK was lost on the way back   → the subscriber already has it → resending duplicates

From the publisher's vantage point these two are indistinguishable. All it sees is silence. So it takes the safe option — resend — and pays for it in duplicates.

That is why the lab's expected duplicate rate is p / (1-p): 25% at a 20% failure rate, 66.7% at 40%. The duplicate rate always exceeds the loss rate, because a break in either leg of the round trip triggers a retransmission.

How QoS 2 removes them

It adds one more round trip so that both sides can agree on when it is safe to forget.

publisher                        broker
   │──── PUBLISH (id=7) ────────▶│  remembers id 7
   │◀─── PUBREC   (id=7) ────────│  "got it"
   │──── PUBREL   (id=7) ───────▶│  delivers, then discards id 7
   │◀─── PUBCOMP  (id=7) ────────│  "done"

The key is the window in which the packet id is remembered. Inside that window, a repeat PUBLISH with the same id is recognised as a duplicate and dropped. After the PUBREL the id is gone, so a repeat gets a PUBCOMP and nothing else.

So exactly-once in QoS 2 is not magic — it is deduplication moved into the protocol. Retransmissions still happen exactly as in QoS 1. They just get filtered.

Whether delivery happens on PUBLISH or on PUBREL is left open by the spec — both implementations are allowed. This lab draws it at PUBREL. Either way the conclusion is the same: filtering repeats of an id while that id is remembered is the whole of the mechanism.

Where the bill lands

QoS 2 is always exactly twice QoS 1 in packets, because it is two round trips instead of one. Latency doubles too — the publisher must cross the network four times before it knows the message is done.

On battery-powered sensors or metered cellular links that difference is the bill, directly. Given that MQTT was designed for exactly those environments, reaching for QoS 2 as a default works against the grain of the protocol.

The most common misconception — QoS is per hop

This is the one people get wrong most often in production. QoS is not an agreement between publisher and subscriber. The publisher↔broker leg and the broker↔subscriber leg each carry their own QoS.

publisher --QoS 2--▶ broker --QoS 0--▶ subscriber
        (set when publishing)   (set when subscribing)

effective delivery quality = the lower of the two

If the subscriber subscribed at QoS 0, then no matter how carefully you published at QoS 2, the message can simply vanish on that last leg. Small comfort that it reached the broker exactly once.

A good share of "I published at QoS 2 and the message never arrived" reports land here. Do not read only the publishing code — check the subscription QoS first.

The "loss" in this lab is not TCP packet loss

One thing to be precise about. MQTT runs over TCP, and TCP already retransmits. A dropped IP packet does not make an MQTT message disappear — TCP resends it and MQTT never learns anything happened.

So when does a message actually go missing at the MQTT level? When the connection breaks. At that instant the messages still in flight, not yet acknowledged, are the ones at risk.

This lab's failure slider compresses that situation into a single probability. In reality losses are not sprinkled evenly like this; they cluster at the moment a connection drops.

Retransmission timing is simplified in the same way. The lab resends on every timeout, but MQTT 5.0 settled on not retransmitting while the connection is alive, sending unacknowledged packets when the session resumes instead. So real duplicates do not scatter finely like this screen — they arrive in a burst right after reconnection.

Can't the DUP flag filter duplicates?

A retransmitted PUBLISH carries DUP set to 1. It is tempting to use that to detect duplicates, and you should not.

DUP only records the sender's situation — "I sent this packet again". If the first attempt was lost, then to the subscriber the DUP=1 copy is the first time it has ever seen the message. Dropping it loses perfectly good data. The same holds in the other direction: the DUP flag on a packet the broker sends to a subscriber reflects the broker's bookkeeping, not the publisher's.

Deduplication has to key on an application-level identifier: put a message id in the payload and have the receiver remember the ids it has seen recently. Which is also the conclusion of the next section.

What to check in production

For most systems the answer is QoS 1 plus idempotent handling. Once receiving the same message twice produces the same result, there is very little reason left to buy QoS 2's second round trip.

-- deduplicate by message id, on the receiving side
INSERT INTO reading (msg_id, device_id, value, at)
VALUES (:msgId, :deviceId, :value, :at)
ON CONFLICT (msg_id) DO NOTHING;

One constraint does what QoS 2 does in the protocol. And it covers more ground — including the case below that QoS 2 cannot reach.

Exactly-once is a promise up to the broker. Suppose the subscriber receives a message, processes it, and dies before sending its acknowledgement. On reconnect, the message arrives again. The protocol kept its promise, but the application did the work twice. No QoS level closes that gap.

QoS only means something if the session survives. Retransmission for QoS 1 and 2 requires the broker to hold session state.

  • MQTT 3.1.1 — connect with cleanSession=false. With true, anything queued is discarded the moment the connection drops
  • MQTT 5.0 — Clean Start=false plus a Session Expiry Interval comfortably longer than your reconnect time. Once it expires you are back to cleanSession=true behaviour
  • A client id that changes on every start (a fresh UUID per restart) can never find its session. Losing messages this way, with QoS dialled up, is a common outcome

Check the subscription QoS. The per-hop problem above. The effective quality is the lower of the publish and subscribe values.

The in-flight limit sets your throughput. QoS 1 and 2 cap how many messages may await acknowledgement (MQTT 5's Receive Maximum). If that cap is small and the round trip is long, throughput stalls with bandwidth to spare. QoS 2 needs two round trips, so at the same cap it delivers half the throughput.

Always bound your retransmissions. This lab stops at 12. At a 40% failure rate that cap actually bites: roughly 0.8% of QoS 2 messages are abandoned undelivered — which is also why measured values fall slightly short of the table above, whose expectations assume unbounded retries. Unbounded retries hammer a failing peer while it is already failing, and a retry loop with no terminal state quietly burns resources until it surfaces as an incident somewhere else entirely.

Roughly how to choose:

situationchoice
Periodic sensor readings; another one is comingQoS 0 — a lost sample is overwritten by the next
State changes, commands, notificationsQoS 1 with idempotent handling
Duplicates are genuinely fatal and cannot be made idempotentQoS 2 — and the limits above still apply

That third row is rarer than it sounds. Most of "this must not be duplicated" is solved more cheaply and more reliably in the application.

This model is simplified for teaching. Real MQTT moves session state, reconnect-time retransmission, in-flight limits and broker queueing policy all at once. In particular, the uniform failure rate here does not match how outages actually behave — real losses and duplicates cluster around the moment a connection drops and comes back.
